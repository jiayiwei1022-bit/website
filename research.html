Job Market Paper

The Real Effects of Anonymous Payment Systems

Solo-authored · [Link]

Abstract

How much anonymity should a payment system provide? Anonymity protects privacy but imposes social costs that have never been measured. I identify and quantify these costs using Bitcoin ATMs as a laboratory. The kiosks convert cash into bitcoin and require identification only above a dollar threshold. After kiosks bring anonymous digital payments to a county, crime rises by 6.9 percent, concentrated in offenses where payments play a central role. The harm extends to health, credit, and employment, falling disproportionately on vulnerable groups such as minority communities and young adults. A comprehensive on-chain analysis sizes the anonymous payment flows behind this harm and yields a social cost of $1.33 per anonymous dollar, roughly an order of magnitude above the fees users pay for anonymity. Classifying billions of transactions, I build the first full-chain anatomy of payment anonymity and characterize the policy design that contains its cost, a cap coordinated across providers.

Working Papers

Who are Better Providers for AI Infrastructure: Evidence from Data Centers

with Yifei Mao and Xuan Tian

Summary. Which owners expand the AI infrastructure available to the economy? With a self-assembled facility-level panel of U.S. data centers and their ownership transaction histories, we find that private equity (PE) acquisitions of colocation facilities raise surrounding business activity, because PE brings the capital and operational capacity to scale computing platforms to what the AI era demands.

Selected presentations: Global AI Finance Research Conference (2026); ABFR Conference (2026)°.

Brokerage Retail Trading and Information Acquisition

Solo-authored

Summary. How does cheaper access to trading affect the market's production of information? After the 2019 elimination of U.S. trading commissions, prices impound more earnings news before announcements. Wholesale market makers internalize retail orders against institutional trades, which lowers institutions' trading costs and strengthens their incentive to acquire information.

Selected presentations: Finance and Product Market Summer School (USI–Lugano, 2024); Economics Letters Macro Finance Seminar (Bocconi, 2024); Wharton–INSEAD Doctoral Consortium (2023).

Banking Without Banks? Cryptocurrency and the De-Banked Cannabis Industry

with Ben Charoenwong and Jan Keil

Summary. Can cryptocurrency substitute for banking when card networks exclude an entire legal industry? Studying the 2022 processor shutdown that cut cannabis dispensaries off from their cashless-ATM debit channel, we find that crypto kiosks serve the excluded merchants but could replace at most about 2 percent of the displaced payment volume, revealing the gap between crypto's promise as banking for the excluded and the capacity it actually delivers.

Geopolitical Risk and Crypto Dollarization

Solo-authored

Summary. What do digital dollars do for households in weak-currency economies? Observing the intermediaries that carry these flows rather than country aggregates alone, I trace how households move into crypto dollars when geopolitical risk rises, how sanctions on named intermediaries reroute rather than stop the flows, and what quantities and prices together reveal about the demand for crypto dollars.

Payback Period and Capital Misallocation

with Olivier Dessaint

Summary. How much capital does the payback period rule misallocate? The rule — widely used by firms to screen investments — discards cash flows beyond a fixed horizon and biases investment against long-lived projects. Combining firm-level data on screening practices with project-level investment outcomes, we quantify the aggregate misallocation this heuristic produces and the characteristics of firms and projects most affected.

Money and the Direction of Science

with Shuping Wu

Summary. How does money shape the direction of science? Tracking scientists' career choices between academic positions and entrepreneurial ventures, we use coauthor networks to trace how funding opportunities reshape which research topics propagate through the scientific community. We ask whether money pulls scientists away from fundamental lines of inquiry and toward commercial applications, and how these shifts aggregate into the direction of science.
